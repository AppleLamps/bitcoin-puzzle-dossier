01

How the puzzle works

One transaction, 256 outputs, a private key per output that lives in a known interval.

Every puzzle is an ordinary pay-to-pubkey-hash address. What makes it a puzzle is that the private key for puzzle N was chosen from a tiny slice of the 256-bit keyspace: an integer between 2^(N−1) and 2^N − 1. Puzzle 1 has a single candidate. Puzzle 20 has about half a million. Puzzle 71, the cheapest still unsolved, has 1.18 sextillion.

01 · PICK A PUZZLE

Choose a puzzle number N. Its address and key range are fixed and public. The ledger tab lists all of them.

02 · ENUMERATE KEYS

Walk the integers in [2^(N−1), 2^N). For each one, multiply the secp256k1 generator by it to get a public key.

03 · HASH AND COMPARE

SHA-256 then RIPEMD-160 the compressed public key. If the 20-byte result equals the address’s hash, you hold the key.

04 · SWEEP THE PRIZE

Import the key into a wallet and move the coins. Do it fast: bots watch the mempool for exactly these spends.

The creator later topped the prizes up to N/100 BTC per puzzle, swept puzzles 161 to 256 back in 2017, and in 2019 revealed the public keys of every fifth puzzle from 65 to 160. Those public-key puzzles can be attacked with Pollard’s kangaroo, which needs roughly the square root of the work of a blind search. Full background on who did this and why is in the case file.

02

How to attempt it

What serious solvers actually run, and what each approach costs.

There is no trick. Every solve so far has been raw computation, so the question is only how many keys per second you can test and how lucky you are.

Address-only puzzles: brute force#71–#160 except every fifth

You only know the address, so each candidate key needs a full elliptic-curve multiplication plus two hashes. GPU tools such as BitCrack, KeyHunt-Cuda and VanitySearch-style kernels do this at a few hundred million to a few billion keys per second per card. Puzzle 71 needs on average 2^69 tests, about 590 quintillion. At one billion keys per second that is around 18,700 years for one card.

A randomized block sweep visits every block once in a seeded order, so it avoids repeats within your run and can resume exactly. It does not change the expected time, and separate browsers can still overlap unless they coordinate their ranges.

Public-key puzzles: Pollard’s kangaroo#140, #145, #150, #155, #160

When the public key is known the problem becomes a discrete logarithm in a bounded interval. Kangaroo (JeanLucPons’ CUDA implementation is the standard) solves an N-bit interval in about 2·√(2^N) group operations. Puzzle 140 needs on the order of 2^71 operations, which is why #130 fell in 2024 to a large GPU pool and #135 fell in July 2026. Puzzle 140 is the current frontier; expect it to take a cluster, not a desktop.

Pools and the front-running problemWhy a found key is not yet a prize

Pools split the interval among contributors and share the reward. Solo or pooled, the dangerous moment is the spend. When your sweep transaction hits the mempool, the public key is revealed, and for puzzles below about 2^125 a well-provisioned bot can derive the private key and replace your transaction with a higher fee before it confirms. Solvers of #66 lost exactly this race in 2024. Broadcast through a private relay or a miner-direct channel, and use a high fee.

05

Bitcoin puzzle FAQ

Short answers to what people search for.

What is the Bitcoin Puzzle Transaction?

A transaction broadcast on 15 January 2015 that funded 256 addresses with 32.896 BTC in total. The private key for puzzle N was chosen from the interval [2^(N−1), 2^N), so each puzzle is exactly one bit harder than the last. The creator later retired puzzles 161 to 256, leaving 160 that can be solved.

How many Bitcoin puzzles are still unsolved?

77 puzzles, #71 through #160, remain unsolved. Together they hold about 903 BTC. The most recent solve was puzzle #135 on 28 July 2026.

How much is the Bitcoin puzzle worth?

Each unsolved puzzle N holds roughly N/100 BTC after the creator's top-ups: #71 holds 7.1 BTC, #101 holds 10.1 BTC, #160 holds 16 BTC. Solved puzzles hold nothing. The unsolved pool is 903.01667813 BTC; the USD figure at the top of this page is computed from a live price.

What is Bitcoin puzzle 71?

Puzzle #71 is the cheapest unsolved puzzle. Its address is 1PWo3JeB9jrGwfHDNpdGK54CRas7fsVzXU, its private key lies between 2^70 and 2^71, and it holds 7.1 BTC. Only the address is known, so it needs brute force over about 1.18 sextillion keys.

How do you attempt a Bitcoin puzzle?

Enumerate private keys in the puzzle's interval, derive each compressed public key on secp256k1, hash it with SHA-256 then RIPEMD-160, and compare with the address. Puzzles whose public key was revealed (#140, #145, #150, #155, #160) can instead be attacked with Pollard's kangaroo, which needs roughly the square root of the work. The web cracker on this site runs the brute-force method in your browser.

Was the 1000 BTC Bitcoin puzzle real?

The "1000 BTC" figure is a myth. The original transaction distributed 32.896 BTC. Later top-ups by the creator raised the per-puzzle prizes to N/100 BTC, and a 2023 deposit added 872.2 BTC across the unsolved addresses, which is where the larger totals come from.

Who created the Bitcoin puzzle?

Unknown. The pseudonymous BitcoinTalk account saatoshi_rising demonstrated control of the puzzle keys in 2017 and is the strongest claimant, but no real-world identity has been established. The case file on this site traces the evidence.

Which Bitcoin puzzles have been solved recently?

#135 fell on 28 July 2026, #69 on 30 April 2025, #68 on 6 April 2025, #67 on 21 February 2025, #130 on 23 September 2024 and #66 on 12 September 2024. The ledger lists every solve date.

The creator is still unknown.

But one pseudonymous account is tied to the puzzle by something stronger than a forum claim: control of the private keys. The public trail points to an early Bitcoin whale and stops there.

01 / PREMISE CHECK

Three facts change the case

What the primary record says before identity theories enter.

The familiar version of the story begins with a user called “Someone,” a thousand-bitcoin giveaway, and a January 15 announcement. None of those three details survives the source check.

1

“Someone” was a mistranslation, not an account

The Russian OP wrote кто-то — “someone” or “somebody” — about whoever launched the transaction. The actual poster was amaclin, who immediately wrote “it wasn’t me.” Verified

2

The first distribution was 32.896 BTC

The transaction spent one 32.9 BTC input into 256 outputs worth 0.001 × N BTC, with a 0.004 BTC fee. The roughly 1,000 BTC figure reflects later 2017 and 2023 additions. Verified

3

The announcement appeared January 23

The funding transaction confirmed January 15, 2015. The earliest public discussion found was the Russian-language Bitcointalk thread posted eight days later. Verified

02 / THE ANNOUNCEMENT

A discovery, not a confession

Bitcointalk topic 932434 · Russian local board

Legendary member amaclin posted “Забавная транзакция (Ханойские башни)” — “Funny transaction (Towers of Hanoi)” — at 07:43:22 forum time on January 23, 2015.

“Забавную транзакцию кто-то запузырил в сеть недавно … Сразу скажу — не я.”

“Somebody recently launched a funny transaction into the network … I’ll say right away — it wasn’t me.”
amaclin · translation from the Russian OPRead the original thread ↗

The thread supplied no creator biography, motive, or formal rules. Amaclin inferred the mechanism from the chain: output N appeared to use a private key in [2^(N−1), 2^N), making each next solution require roughly twice the computation. He published cracked keys 1–24 and cautioned that his own explanation was only a supposition.

What amaclin actually inferred

He described 256 outputs from 0.001 to 0.256 BTC, “clever” randomness, a key range doubling with each output, and a reward that increased without doubling. The “Towers of Hanoi” title and chessboard/rice-grain analogy framed the exponential search space. The thread received two replies on January 25 and then went quiet.

Why the English record starts much later

Bulista opened the English megathread on December 28, 2015 after finding the transaction “while playing around with my bot.” That thread — topic 1306983 ↗ — became the puzzle’s best-known public home. Bulista was a discoverer, not the creator.

06 / CLAIMS & THEORIES

What holds up — and what doesn’t

Evidence-ranked, not lore-ranked.

Early miner / whale

The source wallet’s scale and uncompressed-key predecessor support an early-holder profile. They do not identify a person.

Likely profile

Satoshi Nakamoto

The doubled “a” in saatoshi, double spaces, and “measuring instrument” language are wordplay and stylometry, not identity evidence.

Speculative

Polynomial key pattern

Fitted sequences and “2^n−1” theories overfit early solved keys. Key #4 equals 8, not 15; the key holder explicitly said there is no pattern.

Debunked

amaclin

He denied authorship in the first post, later described the author as unknown, and his own stated holdings conflict with the whale profile.

Not supported

Other “I am the creator” posts

Critical_Thoughts addressed the mystery rhetorically; albert0bsd joked about creating a software bug; Cricktor claimed to solve puzzle #66, not create the series.

Non-claims

A 2022 skeptic correctly argued that a bare forum claim proves nothing and asked for a signed message. No such forum signature appeared. But the July 2017 transaction answered the deeper question: the claimant could spend the puzzle outputs.
